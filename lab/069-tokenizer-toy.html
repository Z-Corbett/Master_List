Language models read text as tokens, not letters. Byte-pair encoding builds a vocabulary by starting from single characters and repeatedly gluing together the most frequent neighbouring pair. Train one here, one merge at a time, then feed it anything.

A TOY. This is an illustrative character-level BPE trained on a few hundred words in your browser. It is not the tokenizer of any production model. Real ones work on bytes, train on vast corpora, and end up with vocabularies of tens of thousands of tokens.

    Rare words shatter A word the corpus never saw gets built from whatever pieces were learned, so it costs many tokens. Common words become a single token, often with their leading space attached.
    Numbers are awkward Digits only merge if digit pairs were frequent in training. Here, with almost no numbers in the story, 1234567 comes out one digit at a time. Real tokenizers make deliberate choices about digits.
    Whitespace is text too Spaces, tabs and newlines are symbols like any other. The pre-tokenizer keeps a single space attached to the following word, which is why “ the” and “the” can have different IDs.
    How it works: text is first split into chunks (a word with its leading space, a run of digits, a run of punctuation, or whitespace), and merges never cross chunk boundaries. Base symbols are the 95 printable ASCII characters plus newline and tab (IDs 1–97), and ID 0 is <unk> for anything else. Each step counts every adjacent symbol pair, weighted by how often its chunk occurs, and merges the most frequent one; ties go to the pair that sorts first. Encoding applies the learned merges in the order they were learned. The built-in story about Wrenmouth is original and fictional.
