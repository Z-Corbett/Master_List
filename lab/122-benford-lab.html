Benford's Law Lab

In many naturally occurring collections of numbers, about 30% start with a 1 and fewer than 5% start with a 9. Pick a dataset or paste your own and test it: first digit, second digit, chi-square and Nigrini's mean absolute deviation.

P(first digit = d) = log₁₀(1 + 1/d)  ·  P(second = d) = Σk=1..9 log₁₀(1 + 1/(10k + d))

χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢ, with Eᵢ = N · pᵢ  ·  5% critical values: 15.507 (df 8), 16.919 (df 9)
MAD = (1/k) Σ |observed proportionᵢ − expected proportionᵢ| over the k digits

With thousands of records the chi-square test rejects almost any real dataset over trivial differences, which is why Nigrini judges conformity by the MAD, which does not grow with N. His ranges: first digit 0–0.006 close, 0.006–0.012 acceptable, 0.012–0.015 marginally acceptable, above 0.015 nonconformity; second digit 0–0.008, 0.008–0.010, 0.010–0.012, above 0.012.

When Benford applies

  • The numbers span several orders of magnitude (tens to millions), so the logarithms spread out smoothly.
  • They arise from growth or multiplication: populations, prices × quantities, compound interest, and the powers, Fibonacci numbers and factorials above.
  • There are enough of them: a few hundred at the least, and ideally over a thousand, before the MAD is meaningful.

When it doesn't

  • Assigned numbers: invoice, account, phone and ZIP numbers are labels, not measurements.
  • Capped or narrow ranges: human heights, test scores out of 100, anything between fixed limits.
  • Built-in minimums and maximums: a $25 fee, a reimbursement ceiling, prices set at $9.99.
  • Primes: their first digits drift toward uniform as the range grows; they follow a different (generalised) law.
  • A failed test is a reason to look closer, never proof of fraud on its own.
