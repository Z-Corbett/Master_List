How it works

In a diffusion cloud chamber, alcohol evaporates from a warm felt at the top and sinks toward a plate cooled well below freezing, often with dry ice. Just above the plate the vapour is supersaturated: it is ready to condense but has nothing to condense on.

A fast charged particle knocks electrons off air molecules as it passes. The ions it leaves act as seeds, and a thin line of droplets forms along its path. The droplets then drift and evaporate, so each track lasts only a second or two.

Reading the tracks

Alpha particles are helium nuclei with charge +2. They ionise heavily and stop within a few centimetres of air, so their tracks are short, thick and straight. Many come from radon and its decay products in ordinary room air.

Beta particles are electrons (or, rarely, positrons). They are light and easily knocked off course, so they leave thin, faint, wandering tracks.

Muons come from cosmic-ray showers high in the atmosphere. They are very energetic and ionise lightly, so they cut long, thin, almost perfectly straight lines. At sea level, roughly one crosses each square centimetre every minute.

About this simulation

A magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways (F = qv × B), so positive and negative particles curve in opposite directions. The radius r = p / (|q|B) is smaller for lower momentum, which is why slowing electrons spiral.

This is an illustration, not a physics engine. Rates, lengths and the field strength (in arbitrary units) are chosen to be visible, and the field is exaggerated: real alpha tracks barely bend in bench-top magnets. The same ?seed= always gives the same run.
