robots.txt Tester

Paste a robots.txt, list some URLs and pick a crawler. Each URL is matched with the rules of RFC 9309 (Robots Exclusion Protocol, 2022), and the page shows which group was chosen, which rule won and why. Nothing is fetched: it all runs on the text you paste.

      Sitemap lines

        Sitemap is not part of RFC 9309's rules; §2.3 allows crawlers to read other records such as it. The line applies to the whole file, not to a group, and should be an absolute URL. A single sitemap may list at most 50,000 URLs and be at most 50 MB (52,428,800 bytes) uncompressed (sitemaps.org protocol); split larger ones and use a sitemap index.

        Read this before blocking anything

        robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing. A disallowed URL can still be indexed (without its content) if other pages link to it. To keep a page out of results, use <meta name="robots" content="noindex"> or an X-Robots-Tag: noindex HTTP header, and leave the page crawlable: if robots.txt blocks it, the crawler never sees the noindex.

        How the matching works (RFC 9309 §2.2):

        • The crawler obeys the group whose user-agent matches its product token, case-insensitively. Groups naming the same token are combined. With no match it uses the * group, and with no * group nothing is blocked.
        • Among the chosen group's rules, the match with the most octets wins. If an allow and a disallow match equally, allow wins. No match means allowed, and /robots.txt itself is always allowed.
        • * matches any run of characters, and $ at the end anchors the end of the path. To match a literal * or $, write %2A or %24.
        • Non-ASCII characters are UTF-8 percent-encoded before comparison, and percent-encoded unreserved characters (letters, digits, - . _ ~) are decoded, so /%62%61%7A equals /baz. Matching is case-sensitive.
        • Crawlers must parse at least 500 KiB (512,000 bytes). Google stops there, and so does this tester: rules after that point are ignored.
        • A 4xx on robots.txt means crawlers may crawl everything; a 5xx or network error means they should treat the whole site as disallowed.
        • crawl-delay is not in RFC 9309. Google ignores it; some other crawlers, including Bingbot, read it.

        Sources: RFC 9309 (rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309) · Google Search Central, "How Google interprets the robots.txt specification" · sitemaps.org/protocol.html
