How an interlocking keeps trains apart

Interlocking
Signals and points are linked so that they can only be set in safe combinations. The idea dates from the mid-19th century, when it was done with mechanical locking in lever frames. Relays followed in the 20th century, and today it is mostly done in software.
Route setting and locking
Setting a route calls its points to the right position and locks them. The signal can clear only when the points are detected in position, every track circuit in the route is clear, and no conflicting route is set.
Track circuits
A low current runs through the rails of each section. A train's wheels and axles short it out, and the section shows occupied. The design is fail-safe: a broken rail or a power failure also shows occupied.
Signal replacement
Once a train has passed a signal, the signal returns to danger automatically, so the next train can't follow it into the route.
Approach locking
If a train is approaching a signal that has cleared, cancelling the route puts the signal back to danger at once, but the points stay locked for a timed period (30 s here) in case the driver has already passed it.
Sectional route release
The route is released behind the train, section by section, as it clears each track circuit. Points unlock as soon as their section is released.
What this model leaves out
Real routes also lock an overlap beyond the next stop signal and use flank protection so that other movements can't run into the side of the route. Real release times, speeds and signal aspects vary by railway. Mesquite Junction is invented.
