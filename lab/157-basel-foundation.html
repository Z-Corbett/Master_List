Foundation

A playable foundation year: eight small studios on seeing, drawing, making and colour.

A foundation course teaches fundamentals before style: how a mark sits in a field, how memory edits what you saw, how edges recede, how white space shapes a word, how a rule grows a plant, how marks become material, how a tile becomes a cloth and how a colour changes with its neighbours.

Each studio has a principle, a prompt and a crit that reads your numbers back to you. When you're done, collect everything onto one A4 sheet.

01

Point, line, plane

Graphic exercises

A square, a dot and a bar are the smallest words in a visual language. Where they sit matters more than what they are: the same few marks can feel settled, restless or rhythmic depending on their place in the field.

The readouts measure what your eye is already judging: where the weight sits compared with the centre, how full the field is, how regular the intervals are, and how hard the marks press against the edges.

Try thisBuild a composition that feels balanced even though its weight is off-centre. Then press Variations and pick the one you would defend in a crit.

02

Memory drawing

Look, then redraw

Drawing from memory trains the eye to look for structure (proportion, placement, overlap) instead of copying detail. You get three seconds; then the picture is gone and only what you noticed remains.

Your redrawing is scored by overlap: the cells you and the original share, divided by all the cells either of you filled (intersection over union).

Try thisBefore you press Start, decide what you'll look for: the largest shape first, then where each other shape sits in relation to it.

03

Perspective

Technical drawing

Parallel edges that recede from you appear to meet at a vanishing point. For horizontal edges that point lies on the horizon, the line at your eye height.

One-point perspective has a single family of receding edges. Two-point turns the box so you see a corner. Three-point tilts your view up or down, so the verticals converge too.

Try thisMove the horizon below the upper box and watch its underside appear. Then pull the two vanishing points close together and notice when the box starts to look distorted.

04

Lettering

Optical spacing and stroke

Letters are spaced by eye, not by ruler. Round and diagonal letters bring white space into the gap, so equal measured distances look uneven: A beside V looks too loose, two straight stems look tight.

The aim is an even amount of white between each pair. The model here measures that white as an area (shown in red), with the deep corners capped, and balances it across the word.

Try thisSpace the word until the gaps look even, then press Compare. Squint: uneven spacing shows up as dark clumps and pale holes.

05

Nature to structure

Nature drawing

Studying a plant is not only about likeness; it is about finding the rule that grows it. A well-known model of a sunflower head turns each new seed about 137.5° from the last (the golden angle) and sets it at a distance that grows with the square root of its number. The seeds pack without gaps, and you can count spirals in two directions.

Leaves have a rule too: veins leave the midrib in a repeating rhythm. Slide from observed to abstract and keep the rule while you drop the accidents.

Try thisNudge the angle half a degree off golden and watch gaps open into spokes. Then set abstraction to full and ask what the drawing still says about the plant.

06

Material and texture

Material studies

Texture is tone made of marks. One shape filled with hatching, stippling, a weave or a grain reads as four different materials, and each can be pushed lighter or darker by density alone.

Rhythm decides whether a texture looks machine-made or made by hand: perfectly regular marks read as mechanical, and irregular ones as organic.

Try thisChange density until all four panels read as the same grey when you squint. Then raise rhythm and see which texture survives irregularity best.

07

Repeat

Textile design

A repeat is a tile that continues into its neighbours. In a straight repeat tiles sit on a square grid; a half-drop moves every other column down by half a tile; a brick repeat moves every other row across by half.

Whatever leaves one edge of the tile has to come back on the opposite edge, shifted to match the repeat, or the seam shows. That is why the tile changes when you change the repeat.

Try thisClick the small tile to drop dots right on its edges, then switch repeats. Turn on the tile outlines and try to find a seam.

08

Colour

Contrast, value, proportion, temperature

A colour is never seen alone. The same grey looks lighter on a dark ground and darker on a light one; this shift is called simultaneous contrast.

Lightness can be stepped evenly in a perceptual colour space such as OKLCH. Proportion decides which colour leads, and warmth is relative: a colour looks warmer or cooler depending on what sits next to it.

Try thisSet the grey so the two squares look as different as possible, then press Reveal. After that, find the proportion at which a small accent holds its own against a large field.
