Spherical navigation

Great Circles

The shortest way between two places on a sphere is an arc of a great circle. On a flat map it bends toward the pole, and on a Mercator chart the straight line is the longer rhumb line of constant heading. Start in Austin and go anywhere.

Why the shortest route looks curved

A flat map has to stretch the sphere somewhere. The equirectangular map stretches every parallel to the length of the equator, and Mercator also stretches north–south by the same factor, 1/cos φ, so that angles are kept. Lines of constant compass heading (rhumb lines, or loxodromes) become straight on Mercator, which is why sailors liked it. The great circle crosses each meridian at a different angle, so on these maps it arcs toward the nearer pole, where the stretched-out distances are really short.

Going east–west along the equator, or due north–south, the two lines coincide. Everywhere else the rhumb line is longer, and for far-apart points at high latitude it can be much longer.

The formulas

a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ sin²(Δλ/2)
d = 2R · asin(√a)  (haversine), evaluated as 2R · atan2(√a, √(1 − a)) with 1 − a = sin²((φ₁+φ₂)/2) + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos²(Δλ/2), which stays exact near antipodes
θ₀ = atan2(sin Δλ cos φ₂, cos φ₁ sin φ₂ − sin φ₁ cos φ₂ cos Δλ)
Rhumb: Δψ = ln(tan(π/4 + φ₂/2) / tan(π/4 + φ₁/2)), q = Δφ/Δψ, d = R·√(Δφ² + q²Δλ²)

A sphere is a model: the WGS-84 ellipsoid differs by up to about 0.5%. Bearings are from true north. Antipodal points have no single great circle, so their bearing and midpoint are undefined.
